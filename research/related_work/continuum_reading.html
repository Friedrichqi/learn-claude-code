Continuum's per-turn bubble

An inference engine frees a request's KV cache the moment decoding ends, because it assumes the request is done. For a ReAct agent, "decoding ended" means "about to call a tool" — and the same 70,000-token context returns about a second later. Continuum prices what that mistake costs, and finds a term nobody had modelled.

01 · The problems it names

End-of-turn eviction, and the bubble it charges every turn

P1

Turn-based eviction re-prefills a very long prefix, about eleven times per program

vLLM and SGLang free a request's KV blocks as soon as decoding ends if anything is waiting. When the tool returns, the engine must redo the full prefill, or reload from DRAM where offloading is on. Their illustrative SWE-agent trace has model steps of 1.3–5.2 s separated by tool calls of 8 ms (sed), 10 ms, and 137 ms (grep) — with one 14 s pytest.

P2

Per-turn queueing delay — the term nobody had modelled

Even when reload is free, an evicted program pays a scheduling bubble every turn, because its next request must re-enter the waiting queue and wait for GPU memory to be released by other requests' in-flight prefill and decode. Three properties make it the paper's real contribution: it survives fast offloading, since bandwidth cannot fix a scheduling problem; it accumulates linearly in turns, exactly the regime agents operate in; and it is invisible to offline profiling, because it depends on live scheduler state — which is precisely why every prior cost model leaves it out. It also destroys program ordering, and raising the returning request's priority does not help, because it is blocked by computation already resident on the GPU rather than by queue position.

P3

Tool durations are long-tailed, so any static "preserve" is unsafe

The slowest 10% of BFCL fetch_url calls account for 52.5% of total delay; the slowest 10% of SWE-Bench cd calls account for 94.1%. Pinning "until the next request arrives" therefore lets one hung tool hold GPU memory indefinitely, and in the limit every block is pinned by a program whose tool has not returned — the scheduling loop deadlocks.

P4

The TTL is a two-sided trap

Too long, and pinned KV blocks other requests and costs throughput. Too short, and the cache is evicted just before the tool returns — so you paid the memory occupancy and still pay the prefill and the bubble, strictly worse than never pinning. This is what forces a real cost model rather than a tuned constant.

One further observation licenses the scheduling half of the design: expected remaining tokens fall as a program advances, on both workloads. So "prioritise programs that arrived earlier, or have executed more turns" approximates clairvoyant shortest-remaining-time-first — which is where the η term in §03 comes from.

02 · Why prior work does not solve it

Everyone has one of the three properties; nobody had all three

The paper reduces the field to three axes — does the system retain KV across the tool pause, does it model per-turn queueing delay, does it bound how long retention lasts. Reproduced from their Table 1:

  • InferCept is the closest work and the one they argue against hardest: right mechanism, wrong policy. Its preserve compares reload cost against GPU occupancy for the immediate next turn only, so with non-blocking LMCache offload reload is cheap and the condition rarely fires — the program is evicted and eats the bubble InferCept never modelled. And the pin is static and unbounded, which P3 turns into memory waste and deadlock. Their verdict: "impractical for real-world deployment."
  • Autellix gets program-level scheduling right but keeps end-of-turn eviction entirely, so it never addresses retention. Its heuristic — longer attained service implies longer remaining — is also empirically wrong on SWE-Bench, where it loses to plain vLLM even though it wins on BFCL.
  • Pie is mechanism without policy: programmable handlers let a developer express custom tool handling, but the developer must hand-design scheduling per agent, and nothing adapts to dynamic tool latency.
  • Parrot, Alto and Teola/Ayo are excluded by construction. All assume a static or deterministically defined DAG, and a ReAct agent's dependency graph only exists at runtime — the next tool depends on the last tool's output.
  • Per-request schedulers — Apt-Serve, AlignedServe, Tempo — optimise individual requests, prefix-aware batching, or mixed-SLO classes, and Cache-Craft reuses chunk caches for RAG documents rather than request-specific agent state spanning a pause.
  • The whole offloading line (LMCache, AttentionStore, CacheGen, CacheBlend, HiCache) is orthogonal, and the paper proves it rather than asserting it: gains hold on top of DRAM offload and on top of SSD offload at both 400 GB and 800 GB.
  • Classical TTL work (DNS, CDN, memcached) bounds staleness over independent entries with semantic correctness constraints. Here entries are coupled through memory pressure, prefill cost and scheduling fairness — hence the claim to be the first system to set a KV TTL from predicted tool duration, scheduling-side delay propagation and workload pattern.

03 · The method

A time-to-live priced in seconds, not tuned as a constant

Everything is denominated in seconds of job-completion latency, so cost and benefit are directly comparable. The cost of pinning is opportunity cost: MemUsage(r)/M is how many average-sized requests get blocked, under the stated assumption that the queue is always backlogged enough for that blocking to be real.

Cost(τ, r) = (MemUsage(r) / M) · τ

Benefit(r) = (MemUsage(r) / M) · Prefill-Reload(r)  +  (T / M) · MemUsage(r) · η

The second term is the one prior policies omit. Prefill-Reload comes from a sub-ten-minute offline profile per model and hardware pair — offload bandwidth, plus a quadratic fit of prefill time against context length. T is a sliding-window average of the queueing delay evicted requests actually experienced, measured online, which is the only way to capture it at all.

τ* = argmaxτ   P(τ, f) · Benefit(r) − Cost(τ, r)

   = argmaxτ   P(τ, f) · ( T·η + Prefill-Reload(r) ) − τ

The shared MemUsage(r)/M factor cancels, which is why the implementation only needs T and P. P(τ, f) is the empirical CDF of tool f's historical durations, and the argmax is solved by enumerating the observed durations — plus τ = 0, meaning "do not pin" — as candidates. Cold start falls back through three tiers with threshold K = 100: per-tool CDF, then a global CDF over all tools, then a fixed default derived from the same model under an Exp(1) duration and η = 1.

The memoryfulness factor is the idea worth stealing

η = −Corr(k, N − k) over requests already served and requests remaining, per program. In one scalar it says: the value of preserving program continuity is exactly how predictable the remaining work is.

η = 1

Fixed turn count. Corr(k, −k) = −1, so preserving order genuinely approximates shortest-job-first and the queueing saving is real.

η = 0

Geometric, memoryless. Expected remaining work is constant regardless of progress, so keeping order buys nothing and the queueing term vanishes.

η < 0

Anti-memoryful. Progress reveals more remaining work, so serve each program briefly and switch often. Designed for; never observed in their traces.

Scheduling and system

  • Priority is a multi-key tuple, in order: preempted requests first, as in stock vLLM; then requests pinned within their TTL window; then program-level arrival time — first-come-first-serve over programs rather than requests, which is what restores the continuity eviction destroys.
  • About 1,000 lines of Python on vLLM. Clients attach a program_id; a Tool-Call Handler runs on request arrival and completion through three hooks, parses the OpenAI function-call schema (for SWE-Bench, the first word inside the bash block), and returns a TTL.
  • Tool duration is measured purely server-side, as the inter-request interval within a program — no client instrumentation. Worth marking: that interval contains everything between the two requests, not only tool execution.
  • Unpinning is scanned at the head of every scheduling step, but skips programs whose follow-up request is already queued, so a cache whose consumer has arrived is not evicted out from under it. Program termination unpins proactively.
  • Deadlock is broken by evicting pinned victims with the latest program arrival time until the head of the queue fits.

04 · Too long, didn't read

The whole paper in one sentence

Continuum observes that an inference engine evicting an agent's KV cache the moment it emits a tool call costs not just a re-prefill but a fresh queueing bubble on every one of about eleven turns — a cost fast DRAM offloading cannot remove and no prior policy models — and fixes it by pinning the cache with a per-tool, cost-modelled time-to-live, balancing GPU occupancy against P(tool finishes in τ) × (re-prefill + queueing delay × how predictable the program's remaining work is), plus program-level FCFS, buying 1.1–3.7× lower job completion time in trace replay and up to 8.18× on a real distributed SWE-agent deployment for about a thousand lines on top of vLLM and a millisecond of scheduling overhead.

05 · Reading against the grain

Eight things to keep straight before citing it

  1. Two very different headline numbers. The abstract's "over 8×" and the introduction's "1.12× to 3.66×" are not the same experiment. The 8.18× is the distributed real-agent run against SGLang and Dynamo, where part of the margin comes from SWE-Bench's 15-minute Docker limit converting latency into scored failures. Cite 1.12–3.66× for the controlled comparison.
  2. Model-coverage mismatch. The abstract advertises "Llama-3.1 8B/70B, Gemma-3 12B, and GLM-4.5 355B". GLM-4.5 appears only in the RL rollout micro-benchmark, never in the end-to-end delay or throughput curves.
  3. Prefill-Reload is approximated as full prefill, ignoring pages that survive in GPU memory. They argue the residue is small under contention; it is acknowledged, not measured.
  4. η is one workload-global scalar estimated from history and applied to every request. The interesting η < 0 regime is designed for but never exercised.
  5. The cost model assumes a backlogged queue. Under light load it overstates the real opportunity cost, so TTLs come out too short — precisely the regime where pinning is nearly free.
  6. Deadlock victim selection inverts the priority rule. FCFS favours early-arriving programs, but when memory runs out the victims are the latest-arriving pins. Self-consistent, but tail programs absorb all the memory pressure and no tail-fairness analysis is given.
  7. Tool duration is inferred from the server-side inter-request interval, so it silently includes client, network and harness time rather than tool execution. This matters a great deal for a harness like ours — see §06.
  8. This is the append-only case. Continuum pins KV across a tool pause assuming the context grows by appending the tool result. It says nothing about compaction, which rewrites the prefix and destroys the value of the pin — the gap our own related-work survey names, and this paper does not close it.

06 · What it changes for us

It names the term our round latency has been paying all along

  • Per-turn queueing delay is the published name for our 3.7 s fixed round term. Our latency breakdown regresses round latency to 3.7 s fixed plus 0.033 ms per uncached token plus 17.4 ms per output token, and roughly 80% of a hosted fault round is fixed provider latency — queue plus a TTFT floor that holds even for a 371-token request. This is the paper to cite for the claim that the floor is scheduling, not compute.
  • Our tool calls are 20–90× shorter than theirs. We measure tools at 10–50 ms against their 925 ms and 1,923 ms means. Shorter pauses push P(τ, f) toward 1 at tiny τ, so on our workload the TTL decision should be close to unconditional — retention is almost free. Continuum's mechanism is more favourable for a Claude-Code-shaped harness than for the agents it was evaluated on.
  • But the harness, not the tool, sets the length of the pause. Our memory_extract step costs 13–21 s per turn — an order of magnitude longer than any tool call in their traces — and it lands inside exactly the inter-request interval Continuum measures as "tool duration". Pinning 70–90k tokens of KV for 13–21 s inverts their cost-benefit: Cost grows linearly in that interval while Benefit is fixed.

The gap worth claiming

Harness-side turn-end work is a first-class input to a TTL policy, and no paper models it. Their η captures the turn structure of the workload; an s15-shaped policy would need a second term for the pause structure the harness itself imposes. Continuum remains the strongest prior art for keeping KV resident across a tool pause — it simply does not touch the two costs we measure after compaction, and it explicitly excludes the asynchronous multi-agent coordination where our teammate-context work lives.
